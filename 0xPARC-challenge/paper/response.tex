\begin{abstract}
The 0xPARC challenges ask how much can be done with a small set of permitted
operations: recover a hidden list, construct a large majority vote, transform
a packed vector and express integer conditions through quadratic equations.
I present possible solutions to all eight questions and examine what their
representations preserve. I use an index-deconvolution method in progress for
the Boolean network, and a CausalBool-assisted Boolean-to-quadratic route for
the arithmetic questions. Index deconvolution provides an exact account of
the Boolean function computed by a majority circuit, including the inputs that
determine its output and the patterns that leave some inputs free. For the Fourier
problem, grouping stages into three multiplication levels reduces the stated
operation cost by factors of 6.347 and 4.743 relative to a dense construction
at the two stated dimensions. For factorisation, explicit bounds make the
modular constraints equivalent to integer multiplication. The main text
explains the constructions through worked examples; Supplementary Information
gives the proofs, complete specifications and verification evidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{What index deconvolution does for each answer}
\label{sec:index-capability}

Index deconvolution takes the complete finite input--output repertoire of a
Boolean system and returns, for each output, the inputs that actually matter
and the gate acting on them. Single-bit perturbations isolate the responsive
coordinates; the restricted behaviour is then matched against a canonical gate
family. The recovered description is replayed forward and must reproduce the
original behaviour exactly. This follows the algorithmic-information-dynamics
practice of using interventions and generative descriptions to expose
structure~\cite{hernandez2019aid,hernandez2021aid}.

A method is only as useful as the claims it actually carries, so each answer
below states which of three roles the method plays. It \textbf{derives} an
answer when the object compiled into that answer is what the method returned.
It \textbf{certifies} an answer when the answer is argued another way and the
method recovers and verifies a decisive Boolean part of it exactly. It
\textbf{bounds} an answer when it does not produce the answer at all and what
it contributes is a measurement of why.

\begin{tabular}{llp{0.56\linewidth}}

Q2 & derives & majority recovered to 151 inputs, identical to an independent threshold\\
Q3 & certifies & the emitted schedule's dependency support, recovered and shown total\\
Q4 & certifies & the solution-set structure for each tractable prime\\
Q5 & derives & the comparator the range predicate is built from\\
Q6 & derives & the comparator and inequality cells at 254 bits\\
Q7 & derives & the full adder and partial product the 64-bit multiplier is built from\\
Q8 & bounds & a measurement of where the representation stops working\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two consequences are worth stating plainly at the outset. The arithmetic cells
are not written down anywhere in the source: each is stated as an integer
relation, handed to the method, and whatever gate comes back is what gets
compiled. For the full adder the method returns sum as \textsc{xor} and carry
as \textsc{majority} without either being named. And Q8's answer is the direct
limb construction of Section~\ref{sec:q8}; the method does not produce it, and
Section~\ref{sec:q8} reports the measurement that explains why. Supplementary
Sections~S10--S11 give the evidence, the denominators and the limits.

\section{Introduction}
The challenges, presented through 3Blue1Brown, bring together several kinds
of restricted computation~\cite{challenge}. A hidden list can be queried but
not read. A majority vote must be assembled from three-input gates. A Fourier
transform has only three multiplication levels available. Integer conditions
must be expressed by equations in a finite field. In each case, the first
question is whether the permitted operations can express the answer. A second
question is what a particular construction costs and how its correctness can
be checked.

I answer the questions in their original order, numbered Q1--Q8. The
mathematical ingredients include positional encoding, majority identities,
Fourier factorisation and binary arithmetic. These have established
precedents~\cite{cohen,cheon}. The contribution of this response is to connect
the solutions to explicit representations that can be checked in both
directions, and to be exact about which direction carries which claim.

Running an answer forwards and recovering it backwards are different acts. A
circuit tells us how to compute an output. Its repertoire is the complete table
of outputs over all input states, and index deconvolution starts from that
table to ask which inputs matter and what function they compute. Where the
recovered description is what we then compile, the method has done the work.
Where the answer rests on a proof over unbounded integers, or on complex
arithmetic, or on the field's own multiplication, it has not, and the table in
Section~\ref{sec:index-capability} says so.

One practical point decides how far any of this reaches. Deconvolution was
originally defined over a materialised repertoire, which costs $2^n$ and caps
every claim built on it at a dozen or so inputs. The same three steps run
instead on an ordered decision diagram, where a canonical representation makes
structural identity the same thing as agreement on all $2^n$ inputs. Essential
variables become a comparison of two restrictions, gate naming becomes an
identity test, and verification becomes an identity test on the recompiled
system. Nothing is sampled and nothing is approximated; what disappears is the
need to write the repertoire down. That is why Q2 below reaches 151 inputs
rather than eleven, and it is also why Q8 does not reach at all.

\section{Recovering a hidden list (Q1)}
Suppose the hidden list contains positive integers $v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1}$.
A query specifies coefficients, and the answer is their exact dot product
with the list. First ask for its sum, $S=\sum_i v_i$. For $n>1$, choose
$B=S+1$ and ask a second question with coefficients $1,B,\ldots,B^{n-1}$:
\begin{equation}
R=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}v_iB^i.
\end{equation}
Each entry is smaller than $B$, so the answer stores the list as base-$B$
digits. Divide by $B$, retain the remainder and repeat. Each remainder is
one entry of the list. Figure~\ref{fig:packing} shows how each entry
contributes to the two answers and how the second answer is unpacked.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/query_network.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{How do two queries recover the hidden entries?}
Panel A gives each entry weight one, returning $S=30$ and allowing base
$B=31$. Panel B weights the same entries by $1,31,961$ and adds their

the quotient continues along the network, while the orange remainder is
one recovered entry. The hidden values are shown to explain the example;
the querying party observes only the two answers. Exact digits are required.}\label{fig:packing}
\end{figure}

If an upper bound $U$ is known, choose $B=U+1$ immediately and use one
query. A one-entry list also needs only one query. For unbounded lists of
length at least two, one fixed rational query cannot suffice: there is a
nonzero integer change to the list whose dot product with the coefficients
is zero. Starting with sufficiently large positive entries gives two
positive lists with the same answer. Thus two adaptive rational queries are
necessary and sufficient.

The phrase ``any list of numbers'' also admits ideal exact real coefficients.
Coefficients linearly independent over the rationals distinguish every
integer list in one query. For example, powers of a transcendental number
have this property. This establishes uniqueness under exact real arithmetic;
it does not give a finite-precision decoding procedure. Fewer questions can
require more information in each answer. Supplementary Section S2 gives the
full argument and its assumptions.

\section{Constructing and recovering majority (Q2)}
A three-input majority gate returns one when at least two inputs are one.
The question asks whether these gates can compute majority on 2025 inputs,
with free duplication of wires. They can. The useful identity is easiest to
understand by temporarily allowing a more general monotone Boolean function
$f$: changing an input from zero to one never lowers its output.

Choose three distinct inputs $a,b,c$. Form three simpler functions by
replacing $b$ with $a$, $c$ with $b$, and $a$ with $c$, respectively. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maj}
f=\Maj\bigl(f_{b\gets a},f_{c\gets b},f_{a\gets c}\bigr).
\end{equation}
If the three input bits agree, all substitutions preserve the assignment.
Otherwise, one preserves it, one increases a bit and one decreases a bit.
Their outputs therefore bracket the original output, with the original
itself among them. Their majority equals $f$.

Each substitution removes an active input. Odd majority is also self-dual:
complementing every input complements the output. Both properties survive
substitution, and a monotone self-dual function on at most two inputs is
just an input wire. Repeating Eq.~\eqref{eq:maj} therefore produces a circuit
containing only three-input majority gates. The proof applies to every odd
input count, including 2025. It gives existence; its loose size bound does
not establish an economical circuit at that scale.

\subsection{A network we can inspect completely}
For five inputs, the construction emits the seven gates in
Figure~\ref{fig:network}. Each arrow carries an input bit or the output of
an earlier gate. For example, $g_0=\Maj(x_0,x_2,x_4)$ and
$g_1=\Maj(x_0,g_0,x_3)$. The final gate takes $g_1,g_3,g_5$.
Reading across a row of the connection matrix identifies the three sources
of that gate; evaluating the gates in order gives the output.

Take input $11100$, written in the order $x_0,\ldots,x_4$. Its first three
bits are one, and its output is one. Now vary the last two bits. The four
inputs $11100,11110,11101,11111$ still have output one, although some
internal gates change. This is the pattern $111**$: either value is
allowed at each star. The network shows how the answer is produced, while
the pattern identifies which variations preserve that answer.

\subsection{What index deconvolution adds}
A circuit describes how to compute an output. Its repertoire is the complete
table of outputs for all input states. Index deconvolution starts from that
table and asks which inputs actually affect the result. For a Boolean
function $f$ on $n$ bits, encode a state by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:index}
\iota(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^ix_i,\qquad
\mathcal I_f=\{\iota(x):f(x)=1\}.
\end{equation}
The index set $\mathcal I_f$ records exactly where the output is one; all
other indices have output zero. Input $i$ matters if there is a pair of
states differing only at $i$ for which the outputs differ. These pairs
isolate the dependence of this output condition inside the complete
repertoire. They do not imply that the integrated network or its evolving
behaviour has been reduced to a smaller table: changing the selected node
union or dynamical condition changes the observation being asked of the same
system. Equality with the original repertoire checks that the stated finite
recovery is exact.

A further compression describes groups of output-one states by patterns,
or \emph{schemata}. Figure~\ref{fig:index} follows the same five-input
network through its ten schemata and all 32 input states. Each schema
fixes three bits to one and leaves two free. Any state with at least
three ones belongs to one or more of these patterns; no state with fewer
ones belongs to any of them.

For $111**$, the fixed ones give decimal anchor $1+2+4=7$.
The \emph{sumandos} are the fillings of the schema's don't-care positions.
Here their numerical offsets are $0,8,16,24$: set neither free bit,
only bit 3, only bit 4, or both. Adding these offsets to 7 gives indices
$7,15,23,31$, the four highlighted rows of the repertoire. The ten
schemata together expand to exactly 16 output-one indices. The union
counts each index once, even when several schemata contain it.

Free positions belong to a particular schema. In $111**$, inputs 3 and 4
can vary without changing the final answer. Both inputs still matter
elsewhere in the repertoire. For example, fixing two of the other inputs
to one makes flipping either one decisive. Likewise, changing $01100$
to $11100$ flips only input 0 and changes the output from zero to one.
These paired states are what index deconvolution uses to recover
functional dependence. The patterns and the perturbation pairs describe
two complementary aspects of the same exact map.

For odd majority on $n$ inputs, every input matters: fix the other $n-1$
bits with exactly $(n-1)/2$ ones, then flip the remaining bit. Complementation
pairs each output-one state with an output-zero state, giving $2^{n-1}$
active indices. This explains why index deconvolution does not remove
inputs from true majority, even though its output set admits schemata with
free positions.

Recovery is not limited to circuits small enough to tabulate. Run on the
decision diagram, deconvolution recovers the emitted circuit at 151 inputs:
every one of the 151 inputs comes back essential, the gate comes back as
majority, and the recovered object is identical to a threshold function built
independently of the majority construction. That identity settles agreement on
all $2^{151}$ input states at once, and not one of them is enumerated. The
diagram holds 41{,}196{,}123 nodes for a circuit of 8{,}116{,}950 gates.

Table~\ref{tab:majority} lists the ladder. It grows as roughly $n^{4}$, which
is what lets the method climb: the running count of active inputs is all a
majority needs to remember, so the description stays small as $n$ grows. Not
every function is so obliging, and Section~\ref{sec:q8} reports one that is
not.

What is recovered is the computed function, not a unique arrangement of
internal gates. Several different circuits can share one repertoire.

Nor are an input count and an output frequency sufficient certificates.
A majority of three disjoint three-input majorities uses all nine inputs
and returns one on half its states, just like true nine-input majority.
Yet groups $110,110,000$ give local votes $1,1,0$ and an incorrect final
one: only four original bits are one. Comparing the actual index sets
exposes this failure. Supplementary Section S4 gives the reconstruction
proof, controls and limits of identifiability.

\section{A Fourier transform within three levels (Q3)}
A Fourier transform rewrites a vector as its frequency components. For
$N$ complex entries, use the unnormalised forward convention
\begin{equation}
(Fx)_r=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}x_j e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}rj/N}.
\end{equation}
The question permits vector additions, rotations and multiplications, but
at most three multiplications along a path from an input to an output.
A usual radix-two factorisation has $\log_2N$ stages. I combine adjacent
stages into three linear transforms, each requiring one multiplication
level. The final transform also restores the natural frequency order.

To implement a linear transform on a packed vector, group its matrix entries
by cyclic diagonal. Each diagonal contributes a rotation of the input,
multiplied entrywise by a known vector of coefficients. Adding those
contributions gives the output. Rotations can be shared: split each offset
into a small step and a multiple of a larger step, reuse the small rotations
and rotate each grouped sum once. Supplementary Section S5 derives the
identity and counts every operation, including the masks and rotations.

Panel A illustrates the three radix-two stages for eight slots, followed

The source states 32,768 complex slots but uses 65,536 entries in its rotation
example. I evaluate both sizes. Charging an addition at $4\,\mu$s, a
known-vector multiplication at $5600\,\mu$s and a rotation at $6100\,\mu$s
gives the comparisons in Figure~\ref{fig:fourier}. Every multiplication by
a known vector consumes one level under this convention.

The selected circuits use 4,757 multiplications, 4,754 additions and 433
rotations at 32,768 slots; the corresponding counts at 65,536 are 13,313,
13,310 and 570. Their estimated serial costs are 29.299516 and 78.083040
seconds. The dense costs are 185.962068 and 370.374740 seconds, respectively:
6.347 and 4.743 times as much estimated work.

All 106 and 121 contiguous partitions into at most three groups were
examined, together with every power-of-two rotation grouping size.
The choices minimise cost within this family. They do not establish a
global optimum over all possible circuits. Executing every operation in
plaintext on 13 test vectors per target size gives maximum normalised
errors below $2.2\times10^{-15}$ against an independent Fourier transform.
Ciphertext noise, key operations and encrypted timing require a separate
implementation study.

\section{When the modular equations force zero (Q4)}
Let $q=2^{127}-1$, the stated prime, and suppose the sums of the first,
second and third powers of $a,b,c,d$ are all zero modulo $q$.
There is no nontrivial solution. From the first equation,
$d=-a-b-c$. Substitution into the third gives
\begin{equation}
a^3+b^3+c^3+d^3=-3(a+b)(a+c)(b+c)=0.
\end{equation}
Since a field has no zero divisors and $q\ne3$, one pair sum must vanish.
Relabel to obtain $b=-a$ and $d=-c$. The square equation now says
$a^2+c^2=0$. If $c\ne0$, division by $c^2$ would make $-1$ a square.
But $q\equiv3\pmod4$, so $-1$ is not a square in this field. Hence $c=0$
and then all four entries are zero. Supplementary Section S6 supplies the
short number-theoretic proof and small-prime examples showing why the
assumptions matter.

\section{Expressing integer conditions by quadratic equations (Q5--Q8)}
These questions use a different, 254-bit prime $P$, specified in
Supplementary Section S1. Arithmetic takes place modulo $P$. A constraint
has the form $A(w)B(w)=C(w)$, where each expression is linear in the
variables $w$. A \emph{witness} is an assignment of all private and auxiliary
values satisfying the constraints.

\subsection{Direct equations and the CausalBool route}
To require a 64-bit integer $x$ (Q5), introduce 64 bits and impose
\begin{equation}
b_i(b_i-1)=0,\qquad x=\sum_{i=0}^{63}2^i b_i.
\end{equation}
The first equation restricts each $b_i$ to zero or one. Their weighted sum
is below $2^{64}<P$, so it represents the intended integer without modular
wraparound. To require $r\ne1$ (Q6), add $(r-1)s=1$. Such an $s$ exists
exactly when $r-1$ is nonzero: it is its multiplicative inverse.

For Q7, write the proposed factors as $u,v$, bound both to 64 bits and
impose $uv=n$ for the public 64-bit integer $n$. Require each factor to be
at least two. Since $uv<2^{128}<P$, the modular product equation is also an
integer equality. A valid factorisation supplies a witness, and a satisfying
witness gives a valid factorisation. These direct constraints verify supplied
factors; they do not provide an algorithm for finding them.

\subsection{Building the same conditions out of recovered gates}
The direct equations above are short because they borrow the field's own
arithmetic. The second route asks a different question: if we are only allowed
Boolean gates, which gates are they, and who chooses them? Here nothing is
chosen by hand. Each cell is stated as an integer relation, its complete
behaviour is handed to index deconvolution, and the gate that comes back is
what gets compiled.

Handed the relation $a+b+c_{\mathrm{in}}$ and nothing else, the method returns
the sum bit as \textsc{xor} on all three inputs and the carry bit as
\textsc{majority} on all three. The classical full adder is recovered rather
than recalled. Handed the one-bit product it returns \textsc{and}. Handed a
running comparison it returns the comparator recurrence, and once the bound bit
is fixed it specialises on its own: against a bound bit of zero it reports that
the outgoing ``less'' depends on the incoming ``less'' alone, because no new
strict inequality can arise there. That specialisation is found, not applied
afterwards.

A recovered gate names a function of any arity, while the constraint layer
carries two- and three-input gates, so the expansion between them is an
obligation rather than a convenience. It is discharged by identity for every
gate the method can return at the arities in use, and for every function of one
to three variables.

Q5 is the range predicate $x<2^{64}$ built from the recovered comparator, over a
decomposition one bit wider than the bound so that out-of-range values are
representable and only the comparator excludes them. Q6 uses the same
comparator at 254 bits together with a recovered inequality cell. Q7 is a
shift-and-add multiplier assembled from the recovered full adder and partial
product, at the full 64 bits the question asks for: 107{,}271 rows over
53{,}572 signals. It accepts genuine factorisations including semiprimes whose
product needs all 64 bits, and rejects trivial factors, wrong products, and a
pair of 34-bit factors whose product overflows 64 bits and would otherwise pass
as its truncated residue. Exhaustive enumeration is complete at the widths
where it is possible: every one of the 299{,}008 triples at widths four, five
and six agrees with an independent integer predicate.

This route is larger than the direct one everywhere, and no efficiency claim is
made for it. What it buys is that the gates doing the arithmetic were derived
from behaviour rather than asserted.

\subsection{A large factorisation and the role of carries}
\label{sec:q8}
A 4096-bit integer exceeds the field modulus, so one field element cannot
hold it. For Q8, split each integer into 64 pieces, or \emph{limbs}, in
base $B=2^{64}$. Each limb is a 64-bit integer:
\begin{equation}
u=\sum_{i=0}^{63}u_iB^i,\qquad
v=\sum_{i=0}^{63}v_iB^i,\qquad
n=\sum_{i=0}^{63}n_iB^i.
\end{equation}
Multiply the limbs and collect terms by position, exactly as in written
multiplication. For each column $k$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:carry}
\sum_{i+j=k}u_i v_j+c_k=n_k+Bc_{k+1}.
\end{equation}
The incoming carry is $c_k$; the outgoing carry is $c_{k+1}$. Set the
initial and final carries to zero, and set output limbs above position
63 to zero. Figure~\ref{fig:carry} illustrates how the column equations
fit together, using decimal digits so every partial product and carry
can be followed by hand. The construction above uses the same pattern
in base $2^{64}$.

The bounds are part of the argument. Intermediate carries are restricted
to 70 bits. Both sides of Eq.~\eqref{eq:carry} are then below $2^{135}<P$,
so the field cannot hide a wraparound in any column. Conversely, ordinary
multiplication produces carries within these bounds. Together with
nontrivial factors and the zero upper outputs, this proves the equivalence
between a satisfying assignment and the intended integer factorisation.

Checks evaluate these equations directly, including altered bits, partial
products, carries and public values. A useful negative control removes the
carry bounds: the false integer statement $2((P+7)/2)=7$ can then satisfy the
multiplication columns modulo $P$. Restoring the bounds rejects it.
Supplementary Sections S7--S10 give completeness and soundness proofs and the
compiled verification record.

\subsection{Where the recovered route stops, and why}
Q8 is answered above, and the recovered route of the previous subsection is not
what answers it. It is worth being precise about why, because the reason is not
effort.

The obvious attempt is to build a 4096-bit multiplier from the same recovered
cells that give Q7. Its cost follows an exact law, fitted over widths four to
sixty-four and confirmed at 128: a width-$w$ multiplier has $7w^2+1$ gates and
$26w^2+12w+7$ rows. At $w=4096$ that is 436{,}256{,}775 rows against 25{,}725
for the limb construction, a factor of about seventeen thousand. Large, but on
its own only an argument about expense.

The real obstruction appears when we ask for the compressed description rather
than the gate count. Deconvolution's reach comes from the decision diagram
staying small, and whether it does is a property of the function, not of the
implementation. Table~\ref{tab:growth} puts the two cases side by side on the
same engine. Majority's description grows polynomially, as $n^{3.96}$ across
six sizes, which is what carries it to 151 inputs. Multiplication's grows by a
factor of $2.977$ for every additional bit, measured across eleven widths, and
is already beyond a budget of eight million nodes at thirteen bits.
Extrapolating that factor to 4096 bits gives a description of some $10^{1942}$
nodes. The observable universe holds about $10^{80}$ atoms.

This is not a gap awaiting a better encoding. The exponential growth of such
descriptions for integer multiplication is a known lower bound~\cite{bryant},
and the measurements above are that bound appearing in our own engine. The limb
construction avoids it entirely by never going through Boolean logic: each limb
product is one field multiplication, and the blow-up has no opportunity to
occur. Reporting the boundary is more useful than omitting the question, since
it says exactly which problems this method is the right instrument for.

\section{What this response establishes}
The solutions give two-query recovery under rational arithmetic, a majority
construction for every odd input count, a depth-three Fourier circuit at
both stated dimensions, a proof that the modular power-sum solution is
zero, and quadratic systems equivalent to the requested integer conditions.
Their assumptions determine what each answer means.

The distinctive addition is the account of what survives each representation,
and of who supplies each answer. Index deconvolution recovers functional
dependence from behaviour and, run on a canonical decision diagram, does so
without writing the repertoire down: majority comes back exactly at 151 inputs,
and the arithmetic cells that build Q5, Q6 and Q7 come back from stated integer
relations with no gate named by hand. The Fourier construction preserves the
transform while making the price of a three-level restriction measurable under
one declared model. The arithmetic construction preserves integer meaning
inside a modular system by bounding every place where wraparound could occur.

The same account marks where the method does not reach. Q8 is answered by the
limb construction and not by recovery, and the reason is measured rather than
conceded: descriptions of integer multiplication grow geometrically in the
width, so at 4096 bits no such description can be written down. A method worth
using is one whose boundary is known, and this response reports both sides of
it.

The finite evidence supports these specific constructions. It includes
small majority circuits, full target-size plaintext Fourier evaluations
and checks of compiled arithmetic constraints. A complete 2025-input
majority circuit, encrypted Fourier timings and generated zero-knowledge
proofs remain outside that evidence. Supplementary Information separates
the general proofs from these finite results and provides the details
needed to reproduce and assess them.
