\section{Questions, conventions, and contribution}
We keep the source's numbering of its eight puzzles~\cite{challenge} and
address five of them: majority (Q2), range (Q5), exclusion of one (Q6), the
64-bit factorisation (Q7) and the 4096-bit factorisation (Q8). List recovery
(Q1), the Fourier circuit (Q3) and the modular power sums (Q4) are omitted,
because index deconvolution does not supply their answers and this document
reports only what it does supply. The constraint field in Q5--Q8 is
$\F_P$, with
\begin{align*}
P={}&21888242871839275222246405745257275088548364400416034343698204186575808495617.
\end{align*}
External field values must be canonical integers in $[0,P-1]$.

This supplement states the exact conventions and gives the full arguments
behind the main text. The Boolean reconstruction is described in Section S3;
the finite verification record is in Section S7.

\section{Q2: majority using only three-input majority}
Let $f$ be a monotone Boolean function and choose distinct active variables
$a,b,c$. Write $f_{b\gets a}$ for the function formed by substituting $a$ for
$b$, and similarly for the other identifications. Then
\[
 f=\Maj(f_{b\gets a},f_{c\gets b},f_{a\gets c}).
\]
If the three bits agree, every substitution leaves them unchanged. Otherwise
one substitution leaves the assignment unchanged, one increases a bit, and
one decreases a bit. By monotonicity the three outputs are $f$, a value at
least $f$, and a value at most $f$. Their majority is $f$.

Odd-input majority is monotone and self-dual: complementing all input bits
complements its output. Identification preserves both properties. A monotone
self-dual Boolean function on at most two active variables is a projection:
its values at $00,11$ are $0,1$, and the values at $01,10$ are opposite.
Thus recursion terminates in input wires and uses only $\Maj$ gates.

The construction represents subproblems by a positive odd total of weights
over the original coordinates. Identification merges weights, a weight greater
than half the total immediately returns its input, and otherwise the first
three active variables are used. Memoisation identifies equal weight tuples.
Every operand is an external input or an earlier gate; ordered duplicate ports
and free fan-out are permitted. Constants and inversions are absent.

With $T(2)=0$ and $T(k)\le1+3T(k-1)$, a loose unshared bound is
$T(n)\le(3^{n-2}-1)/2$ for $n\ge3$. This proves the requested existence for
2025 inputs but is not a compactness guarantee. No 2025-input circuit is
materialised here. Gate counts below refer only to emitted circuits. Exhaustive
checks cover odd sizes through 11. Beyond that, enumeration is replaced by
identity on a canonical decision diagram, which settles agreement on all $2^n$
states without visiting any: the emitted circuit is confirmed equal to an
independently built threshold recurrence at $n=13$, $15$, $21$, $31$, $41$,
$51$, $63$, $101$ and $151$. At $n=151$ this is 8{,}116{,}950 gates and
41{,}196{,}123 decision nodes. The threshold target shares no code with the
majority builder.

For comparison, a naive majority of disjoint group majorities fails: the
groups $110,110,000$ produce local votes $1,1,0$ and hence output one, although
only four of the nine original inputs are one.

\section{Index deconvolution: representation, recovery and controls}
\subsection{Exact index sets and schema expansion}
Let $f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$ be a deterministic output column. States are
ordered by $\iota(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^ix_i$, with bit zero varying fastest.
The active index set $\mathcal I_f$ consists of all indices with output one.
Membership in this set specifies the complete output column.

For a schema $\sigma\in\{0,1,*\}^n$, let $J_1(\sigma)$ be its fixed-one
positions and $J_*(\sigma)$ its don't-care positions. Define the decimal
anchor and the offset family by
\[
 a_\sigma=\sum_{i\in J_1(\sigma)}2^i,\qquad
 \Omega_\sigma=\left\{\sum_{i\in J_*(\sigma)}2^i z_i:z_i\in\{0,1\}\right\}.
\]
The sumandos are the fillings of the schema's own don't-care positions;
$\Omega_\sigma$ is the numerical offset family these fillings generate.
The indices represented by the schema are exactly
$a_\sigma+\Omega_\sigma$. Fixed-one and free positions are disjoint, so
adding their encodings cannot create a carry. A covering family $\mathcal C$
represents the output precisely when
\[
\mathcal I_f=\bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathcal C}(a_\sigma+\Omega_\sigma).
\]
This is a union, since schemata may overlap. One must retain each anchor's
associated offset family; arbitrary cross-pairing of anchors and offsets
need not preserve the function. A cover by complete assignments always
exists, but a compact cover or a minimal cover is not guaranteed.

For three-input majority, $\mathcal C=\{11*,1*1,*11\}$ gives anchor/offset
pairs $(3,\{0,4\})$, $(5,\{0,2\})$, $(6,\{0,1\})$. The expansion is
$\{3,5,6,7\}$. All three inputs affect the function, although each schema
has a free position. Disconnected coordinates are free in every schema;
freedom in one schema does not imply disconnection.

\subsection{Isolation of functionally responsive coordinates}
From the complete column, recover
\[
I_c(f)=\{i:\exists x,\ f(x)\ne f(x\oplus e_i)\},
\]
where $x\oplus e_i$ flips bit $i$. These are the functional inputs. If
$i\notin I_c(f)$, changing only bit $i$ never changes the output. Any two
states agreeing on $I_c(f)$ can be joined by a sequence of flips outside
$I_c(f)$, so their outputs agree. For a static one-output projection this is
an exact consistency identity, but the inverse method uses it to isolate an
output condition within the full integrated description; it does not discard
the remaining nodes or claim to produce a reduced truth table for the whole
system. In a dynamical network, the relevant observation is instead indexed
by the selected node union, state and time or intervention condition. Different
selections can expose different outputs of the same evolving system. A
declared model family may still be used to name an isolated pattern, but
multiple names or internal implementations can describe the same behaviour.

Testing all coordinate pairs costs at most $n2^{n-1}$ comparisons per
output column, with $O(2^n)$ storage when the full column is retained.
The method does not remove the exponential cost of supplying an arbitrary
complete repertoire. A compact schema family is a representation benefit,
not a universal guarantee that this inverse computation is inexpensive.

For a network with observed outputs for every input state, apply the same
argument to each output column to recover a functionally equivalent map.
Structurally declared but functionally inactive inputs are not identifiable.
If only a circuit's final output is observed, internal gates and their
wiring are not identifiable either. Partial or noisy observations do not
satisfy this exact-recovery premise.

\subsection{Majority checks and negative controls}
For odd $n$, setting exactly $(n-1)/2$ of the other bits to one shows that
every input of majority is functional. Complementation gives exactly
$2^{n-1}$ active indices. The added manuscript check evaluates the emitted
circuits for $n=1,3,5,7,9,11$, applies functional-input detection and column
reduction, and compares the result with the independent condition
$\sum_i x_i>n/2$ on every state. This covers $2+8+32+128+512+2048=2730$
states. The check also expands the three schemata above exactly.

A free-input control embeds three-input majority in five coordinates. All
32 states recover $I_c=\{0,1,2\}$ and the eight-entry majority table;
coordinates 3 and 4 do not affect the output. An incorrect-circuit control
uses a majority of three disjoint three-input majorities. It has nine
functional inputs and 256 active indices, yet its full output column differs
from nine-input majority. Thus neither the number of functional inputs nor
the size of the active set establishes equality; exact membership is checked.
The main text's $110,110,000$ assignment is one explicit disagreement.

The worked network uses the emitted five-input circuit, with

g_2&=\Maj(x_1,x_3,x_4),&g_3&=\Maj(x_0,x_1,g_2),\\

\section{Q5 and Q6: range and exclusion constraints}
\subsection{What was stated, and what the method returned}
\label{sec:cells}
The cells behind Q5, Q6 and Q7 were obtained by one procedure, described here
once and used by all three. For each cell an integer relation is written as a
map from an input bit vector to an output bit, with nothing said about how the
map is to be computed. The relation is evaluated over its complete input space,
the resulting behaviour is turned into a decision-diagram root, and that root is
handed to index deconvolution, which reports the coordinates the output
actually responds to and matches the restricted behaviour against the canonical
gate family of Section S3. The returned gate is what the compiler receives. No
gate literal appears in the builders that assemble the comparison scan, the
inequality scan or the multiplier; every gate they place is read from a
recovery record.

For the range and exclusion questions the stated relations are two scan steps.
The comparison step has inputs $(\ell,e,x)$, the incoming ``less'', the incoming
``equal'' and the value bit at this position, and a bound bit $\beta$ which is a
constant of the position rather than an input. Its two outputs are stated as
\[
 \ell_{\mathrm{out}}=\ell\vee(e\wedge\bar x\wedge\beta),\qquad
 e_{\mathrm{out}}=e\wedge[x=\beta],
\]
which is the meaning of a running comparison and not a circuit for it. The
inequality step has inputs $(d,x)$ and a constant bit $\gamma$, with the single
output $d_{\mathrm{out}}=d\vee[x\ne\gamma]$. Because $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are
constants of the position, each step is recovered twice, once for each value.

Table~\ref{tab:cellrecord} is the recovery record as the method produced it.
The connected inputs are the coordinates found essential by the cofactor test;
the gate is the canonical name and the match class is the full set of families
whose exact behaviour agrees, so that the name may be read as the choice within
that class which it is.

Read as expressions, the record says
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
 \beta=1:&\ell_{\mathrm{out}}=\ell\vee e\bar x,\qquad e_{\mathrm{out}}=e\wedge x,\\[2pt]
 \beta=0:&\ell_{\mathrm{out}}=\ell,\qquad\qquad\ \ e_{\mathrm{out}}=e\wedge\bar x,\\[4pt]
 \gamma=0:&d_{\mathrm{out}}=d\vee x,\\[2pt]
 \gamma=1:&d_{\mathrm{out}}=d\vee\bar x.
\end{array}
\]
The two clauses of the $\beta=1$ ``less'' cell are returned as a regulatory
disjunctive form with $\ell$ as a lone activator and $e$ activating under the
inhibition of $x$, which is exactly $\ell\vee e\bar x$; the $\gamma=1$ cell is
returned the same way as $d\vee\bar x$. At $\beta=0$ the method reports a single
connected input for ``less'' and names the unary gate, which is the wire itself:
two of the three stated dependencies are absent from the behaviour, because
below a zero bound bit no new strict inequality can arise. This specialisation
is a finding of the cofactor test, not a simplification applied to a circuit
afterwards; no circuit exists at that point to simplify.

A recovered gate names a function of whatever arity it found essential, while
the lowering carries two- and three-input gates, so the expansion between them
is a proof obligation. It is discharged by root identity: the expanded network
is evaluated back into a root, the named gate is built independently by the
engine's own gate builder, and the two roots are compared. All nine expansions
in use are checked this way, together with every candidate the identifier can
return at arities one to five and every function of one to three variables.

\subsection{From recovered gates to quadratic rows}
A quadratic row means $A(w)B(w)=C(w)$ in $\F_P$, with all three expressions
linear in the signals. Both relations are compiled from the cells above, and
the two field-level equations they carry are consequences of putting Boolean
wires into a field rather than a separate construction. A wire holding a bit
must be pinned to $\{0,1\}$, which one quadratic row expresses uniquely, and
the integer must be tied to those bits:
\[
 b_i(b_i-1)=0\ (0\le i<w),\qquad x=\sum_{i=0}^{w-1}2^ib_i.
\]
Every satisfying bit is zero or one because a field has no zero divisors.
For a 64-bit range the sum lies in $[0,2^{64}-1]\subset[0,P-1]$, so its
canonical representative equals $x$ as an integer. Conversely the ordinary
binary expansion of any such $x$ provides a witness. A field circuit cannot
distinguish $x$ from an external integer $x+P$; canonical input validation is
therefore explicit. The compiled Q5 system takes the decomposition one bit
wider than the bound, so that an out-of-range value is representable and the
recovered comparator is what rejects it; the bit count alone never does the
work.

\subsection{Q5: the range predicate}
The predicate is built by threading $(\ell,e)$ through the recovered comparison
cell from the most significant position down, initialised with $\ell$ false and
$e$ true, and reading the final ``less''. At each position the scan selects the
cell recovered for that bound bit; the scan itself contains no logic beyond that
selection. Compiled at 65 bits against the bound $2^{64}$, the system is 335
rows over 200 signals.

The scan is exact at every width where it can be enumerated completely. For
each width $w$ from one to eight, and each of the $2^w$ bounds, the predicate
was rebuilt from the recovered cells and evaluated on all $2^w$ inputs against
the integer test $x<b$. This is $\sum_{w=1}^{8}4^w=87{,}380$ cases, and all
87{,}380 agree. What is not claimed is compiled-circuit verification: the
pinned Circom toolchain is absent from the recorded environment, so that status
is UNKNOWN rather than PASS, as Section S7 records.

\subsection{Q6: exclusion of one}
The exclusion is compiled from the same comparison cell run at 254 bits against
$P$, which forces the element to be canonical, together with the recovered
inequality cell scanned against the constant one. The system is 2{,}193 rows
over 1{,}223 signals. Valid witnesses are checked at $r=0,2,P-1$. The forgery
worth naming is the modular alias: an assignment claiming $r=1$ while carrying
the bit pattern of $P+1$, which is the same field element written
noncanonically. Injected directly into the compiled rows, it is rejected by 102
of them, every one a gate row of the recovered scan, so it is the recovered
cells themselves that refuse it. Rejection is read from the rows, never from a
witness generator declining to produce an assignment.

A shorter relation is available to the field directly, $(r-1)s=1$, which has a
witness exactly when $r\ne1$, since one may take $s=(r-1)^{-1}$ while at $r=1$
the equation would read $0=1$. It is one row over two signals. It is retained
as an independent cross-check on the compiled system rather than as the answer,
and the size difference between them is reported plainly in Section S7.

\section{Q7: a 64-bit factorisation}
\subsection{The multiplier built from recovered cells}
The two cells this question needs are the last two rows of the experiment of
Section~S4.1. The full adder is stated as the integer $a+b+c_{\mathrm{in}}$,
with its low bit and its high bit taken as the two outputs; the partial product
is stated as the one-bit product $uv$. Enumerating the first over its eight
input states and the second over its four, the method returns all three inputs
essential for both adder outputs, the sum as XOR and the carry as MAJORITY,
and the product as AND. That is the classical full adder, and neither name
occurs in what the method was given.

The multiplier is then a shift-and-add schedule over those two cells and
nothing else. For each factor bit $j$ the schedule places one recovered partial
product per column in range and one recovered full adder per column of the
running total, threading the carry along the column and retaining the stage
carry at the end. No product bit is discarded by construction, and the stage
carries are carried out of the network as outputs so that the compiler can
constrain them to zero; the absence of truncation is therefore an explicit
constraint rather than a property of the schedule. Compiled at the full 64 bits,
the system is 107{,}271 rows over 53{,}572 signals.

\subsection{The exhaustive ladder and the law it fixes}
Verification is exhaustive wherever exhaustion is possible and adversarial where
it is not.

At the Boolean level the multiplier was enumerated at widths one to five. For
every pair $(u,v)$ the complete $2w$-bit product must equal $uv$ and every stage
carry must be zero: $4+16+64+256+1024$ pairs, with no failure.

At the constraint level the serialized rows themselves were swept. For widths
four, five and six, every triple $(u,v,n)$ over the width was evaluated against
the rows and against the independent integer predicate $u\ge2\wedge v\ge2\wedge
uv=n$, and the two were required to agree in both directions --- a satisfying
triple must satisfy every row, and a non-satisfying triple must break at least
one. Table~\ref{tab:ladder} records the sweep. There is no discrepancy in
299{,}008 triples.

At 64 bits enumeration would require $2^{192}$ triples and is not attempted.
Instead the released system is attacked. It accepts genuine factorisations,
including semiprimes whose product occupies all 64 bits, the largest accepted
product being $18{,}446{,}743{,}979{,}220{,}271{,}189$. It rejects an all-zero
assignment, a trivial factor in either position, a wrong public product, and a
pair of 34-bit factors whose product overflows 64 bits and would otherwise pass
as its truncated residue. The forged assignment recorded in the evidence keeps a
valid pair $(u,v)=(3,5)$ while claiming $n=0$, and four rows reject it.

\subsection{The direct system, retained as a cross-check}
The field can state the same condition in 200 rows. Range-constrain public $n$
and private $u,v$ to 64 bits, enforce $uv=n$, and enforce $(u-1)s=1$ and
$(v-1)t=1$. The system also explicitly enforces $u,v\ge2$ using an inverse for
the sum of each factor's bits above position zero. That sum lies between 0 and
63, so its nonzero field value means the factor is at least two. These extra
rows make the public-input promise robust.

Completeness follows by taking the binary expansions and inverses from every
nontrivial integer factorisation. For soundness, range constraints give
$0\le u,v<2^{64}$, so $uv<2^{128}<P$. Both $uv$ and $n$ are therefore
canonical integers, and the field equality lifts to $uv=n$ over the integers.
The inverse constraints exclude trivial factors. The circuit checks supplied
factors; it does not find them.

Both routes are at width 64, so the comparison between them is like for like:
107{,}271 rows against 200, a factor of 536. The direct system borrows the
field's own multiplication and the recovered one borrows none of it, which is
the whole of the difference. No efficiency claim is made for the recovered
route; what it establishes is that the gates performing the arithmetic were
obtained from behaviour rather than asserted.

\section{Q8: a 4096-bit factorisation}
Put $B=2^{64}$ and represent $u,v,n$ by 64 limbs each:
$u=\sum_i u_iB^i$, $v=\sum_i v_iB^i$, and $n=\sum_i n_iB^i$.
Range-constrain every limb, including public $n_i$, to 64 bits. Introduce 4096
partial-product signals and 129 carries, with rows
\begin{align*}
 u_iv_j&=q_{ij} &&(0\le i,j<64),\\
 \sum_{i+j=k}q_{ij}+c_k&=n_k+Bc_{k+1} &&(0\le k<128),\\
 c_0&=c_{128}=0,
\end{align*}
where $n_k=0$ for $k\ge64$. Constrain $c_1,\ldots,c_{127}$ to 70 bits.
For each factor take the sum of its 4095 bits other than global bit zero and
require an inverse of that sum. Since $4095<P$, this enforces that the factor
is at least two.

\paragraph{Completeness.}
Every integer factorisation $n=uv<2^{4096}$ with $u,v\ge2$ has factors below
$2^{4096}$. Use their base-$B$ limbs, exact partial products, and ordinary
schoolbook carries. Inductively $c_k\le64(B-1)$: with at most 64 products,
the column total is at most
$64(B-1)^2+64(B-1)=64B(B-1)$. Hence $c_{k+1}\le64(B-1)<2^{70}$.
The output fits 64 limbs, so all higher output limbs and the final carry are
zero. Binary decompositions and the indicated inverses complete the witness.

\paragraph{Soundness.}
Every $u_iv_j<B^2=2^{128}<P$, so each partial-product field equation fixes
the exact canonical product $q_{ij}$. The left side of any column is at most
$64(B-1)^2+(2^{70}-1)$; the right side is at most
$(B-1)+B(2^{70}-1)$. Both are nonnegative and less than $2^{135}<P$.
Thus every column equality is an integer equality, not merely a congruence.
Multiplying column $k$ by $B^k$ and summing cancels the intermediate carries,
giving $uv=n+B^{128}c_{128}-c_0=n$. The zero upper outputs prevent overflow
past the 64 public limbs, and the inverse constraints make both factors
nontrivial. This proves both directions independently of witness generation.

\paragraph{Why the recovered route does not reach this question.}
The construction above is what answers Q8, and index deconvolution is not what
produces it. The reason is measured rather than asserted, and it is worth
separating two different costs.

The first is the gate count of a bit-level multiplier, which would be the
obvious way to extend the Q7 route of Section~S5 to this width. Its scaling is
the exact law fitted there, so $w=4096$ gives 436{,}256{,}775 rows against
25{,}725 here, a factor of about seventeen thousand. That is an argument about
expense only.

The second is decisive. The method's reach depends on the decision diagram
staying small during the recovery, and whether it does is a property of the
function rather than of the implementation. The quantity to compare is the
allocation, every node the recovery had to hold, and it must be compared with
allocation rather than with the recovered function's own description, which is a
different and much smaller thing. Built on the same engine, majority's
allocation grows as $n^{3.96}$ across six sizes from $n=31$ to $n=151$, reaching
41{,}196{,}123 nodes at $n=151$; that is why Section~S3's recovery climbs so
far, and the recovered function's own diagram there is only 5{,}776 nodes. The
multiplier's allocation grows by a factor of $2.977$ per additional bit,
measured across eleven widths from two to twelve, where it already occupies
4{,}384{,}542 nodes and exceeds an eight-million-node budget at thirteen. The
per-bit ratios settle quickly --- $5.778$, $4.721$, $3.688$, $3.347$, $3.094$,
$3.019$, $2.983$, $2.973$, $2.958$, $2.953$ --- so the extrapolation rests on a
rate that has stopped moving rather than on a trend still in transient.
Extrapolating it to 4096 bits gives roughly $10^{1942}$ nodes, against about
$10^{80}$ atoms in the observable universe. At no point in that ladder does the
recovered description become available to compare, because the recovery does not
finish.

This is not an encoding that has yet to be found. Exponential growth of such
descriptions for integer multiplication is a known lower bound~\cite{bryant},
and the measurements are that bound appearing in our own engine. The limb
construction escapes it by never entering Boolean logic at all: each limb
product is a single field multiplication, so the blow-up has no opportunity to
arise. Recording the boundary states which problems this method is the correct
instrument for, which omitting the question would not.

\subsection{Why the omitted-bound controls are informative}
For the large-factor carry control, take $u=2$, $v=(P+7)/2$ and public
$n=7$. The ordinary product is $P+7$. Write the factors and $n$ in limbs,
set $c_0=0$, and compute successive field carries by
\[
c_{k+1}=B^{-1}\left(\sum_{i+j=k}u_iv_j+c_k-n_k\right)\pmod P.
\]
All multiplication columns are then satisfied. Summing the weighted columns
shows that $c_{128}=B^{-128}(uv-n)=0$ in the field, so even zero endpoints
cannot reject the false integer equality by themselves. The released system
rejects these carries because of its 70-bit range equations. Deleting those
range equations makes this assignment pass, isolating their role.

The small-factor control uses the same modular alias and isolates the
64-bit factor bound. A public-limb control replaces $n_0$ by $n_0+B$ and
$n_1$ by $n_1-1$, leaving the represented integer unchanged but violating
the canonical limb range. A separate overflow control attempts to represent
$2^{4095}\cdot2$ with all public limbs zero. These controls check distinct
ways in which a field assignment could violate the intended integer statement.

\section{General capacities and developmental scope of index deconvolution}
\label{sec:index-capacities}

The index-deconvolution method is the Boolean-network component used in this work.
Its conceptual lineage is the algorithmic
information-dynamics and programmability framework developed for analysing
how integrated systems respond to interventions, how generative rules expose
their behaviour, and how such descriptions can support explanations and
predictions~\cite{hernandez2019aid,hernandez2021aid,zenil2019calculus,
zenil2019deconvolution}. The method is strongly aligned with the
Kolmogorov--Chaitin view of information as program size and with its modern
formal treatment~\cite{kolmogorov1965,chaitin1966,li2019}. In parallel, its
interest in how simple rules can generate rich behaviour is consonant with
Wolfram's Principle of Computational Equivalence, namely the proposed
computational equivalence in sophistication of many processes above a low
threshold~\cite{wolfram2002,wolfram2002pce}. We use that principle as a
conceptual motivation, not as a theorem about the present Boolean examples.
The perturbational and causal interpretation follows Zenil and collaborators'
model-based interventional calculus and causal deconvolution work~
\cite{zenil2017causality,zenil2019calculus,zenil2019deconvolution}. The
present method is a specialisation of these ideas to exact finite Boolean
descriptions, and remains distinct from a computation of Kolmogorov
complexity itself.

\subsection{The capacity map}

For a synchronous Boolean network, the forward index-deconvolution representation
maps a connectivity matrix and node rules to an ordered repertoire. The
inverse method treats the network and its evolution as one integrated system,
rather than as a collection of independently reduced tables. Given node sets
$S_1,\ldots,S_m$, it can ask for a pattern associated with their union
$U=\bigcup_{r=1}^{m}S_r$ under a stated dynamical condition, such as an
initial state, time window, intervention or output coordinate. For the
single-output illustration used in the checked Boolean example, a coordinate
$i$ has an output-changing witness exactly when there is a state $x$ such that
\begin{equation}
  O_k[x]\ne O_k[x\mathbin{\oplus}2^i].
\end{equation}
Such a witness isolates a dependence under the stated condition; it does not
cut a piece out of the integrated dynamics. Changing $U$ changes the
observation and conditioning map, and can therefore change the set of outputs
that is obtained. The isolated pattern is consequently not asserted to be a
set-theoretic subset, intersection, or independent sub-behaviour of the whole
system. It is an exact observation of the integrated evolution under the
specified node union and condition.

When the supplied description contains the complete transition map, its
evolution can be iterated from every admissible state. A lossless pattern
description can then account for all outputs and attractor cycles entailed by
that description, including those seen through different unions of node sets.
This attractor statement requires a transition description; the static
single-step repertoire checked here supports exact output and perturbation
claims but is not by itself a dynamical attractor analysis.

The main capacities and their exact boundaries are summarised below. The
word ``exact'' always refers to the stated finite model and its supplied
repertoire; it does not mean that an arbitrary hidden implementation is
uniquely identifiable.

\subsection{Compression and the Kolmogorov perspective}

The schema representation makes the algorithmic-information connection
concrete. A schema fixes some coordinates and leaves others as don't-cares.
This is a fixed, exact code for the supplied finite behaviour, whereas
Kolmogorov complexity is defined by the shortest program relative to a fixed
universal description language~\cite{kolmogorov1965,chaitin1966,li2019}.
The distinction is important when comparing the present decoder with the
Block Decomposition Method (BDM), which uses algorithmic-probability/CTM
estimates on local blocks and combines them into a global complexity estimate
~\cite{zenil2018bdm}. BDM is a general approximate complexity framework; the
index-deconvolution schemas here are an exact finite-domain representation,
not a BDM estimate and not a direct evaluation of $K$.

Its fixed coordinates determine a decimal anchor $P$; the fillings of its
own don't-care positions form the sumando family $S$. Across a decimal family
$L$ of anchors, the decoder expands the representation by
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Dec}(L,S)=\{\ell+s:\ell\in L,\ s\in S_{\ell}\}.
\end{equation}
The expansion is checked against the original output-one index set, so this
is lossless representation rather than a probabilistic approximation. When a
function has repeated structure or overlapping regularities, the schema
description can be substantially more compact than listing every active
index. For functions such as XOR, however, the same representation can offer
little or no compression. Compression is therefore a capacity to be measured
for a specified function, not a property to be assumed in advance.

Kolmogorov complexity $K(x)$ is the length of the shortest halting program
that produces $x$ and is uncomputable in general~\cite{kolmogorov1965,
chaitin1966,li2019}. A fixed index-set encoding therefore supplies a
computable description length, and hence an upper-bound candidate relative to
that encoding, rather than $K(x)$ itself. The method does not establish
minimality across all programming languages, coding schemes or
representations. Its algorithmic character comes from describing an
individual deterministic object by a generative rule and testing whether
that rule reproduces the object exactly. This is the sense in which the
method is compression-oriented while retaining a rigorous distinction between
an exact decoder and an estimated algorithmic-complexity value.

\subsection{Prediction, explanation and scale}

Once a pattern has been isolated under a specified node union and dynamical
condition, the integrated description can be replayed for a new admissible
condition without consulting the original hidden implementation. Schema
membership gives the same answer for an input in the represented domain,
while a paired state $x\mathbin{\oplus}2^i$ supplies a direct witness for
whether coordinate $i$ can affect the observed output. In this limited but
precise sense, the method supports prediction and explanation together: the
predicted output is accompanied by the selected condition, the integrated
generative description, and the exact replay check. This is the model-driven
explanatory role that motivates the perturbation framework in the earlier
work~\cite{hernandez2019aid,hernandez2021aid,zenil2019calculus,
zenil2019deconvolution}.

The computational boundary is equally important. Given the full
$2^n\times n$ repertoire, exhaustive paired checks for all columns take
$O(n^2 2^n)$ time, but obtaining and storing that repertoire already costs an
exponential number of states. Exact inversion is therefore practical for
small networks and for larger systems only when an additional structural
condition, such as bounded locality, makes the relevant observations
effectively complete. Partial, noisy or non-recurrent observations require a
different approximate problem and are not silently treated as exact
deconvolution.

The method remains in development. The current implementation establishes
finite exact reconstruction and lossless schema expansion for the checked
Boolean cases; it does not yet provide a universal minimal description,
representation-invariant complexity value, unique recovery of internal gate
wiring, or a general out-of-sample predictor. Larger-network trajectories,
partial observations, richer dynamical systems and principled comparisons of
description lengths remain development directions. These qualifications are
part of the method's scientific specification rather than caveats added after
the result.

is a property of the act of recovering it.

\subsection{Two growth rates, and why they differ}

Fitted over $n=31$ to $n=151$, the allocation grows as $n^{3.96}$ and the
description as $n^{1.97}$. The description obeys a closed form,
\begin{equation}
  \lvert\text{diagram}\rvert=\frac{(n+1)^2}{4},
\end{equation}
which is verified at every size in the ladder rather than fitted, and which the
certificate asserts on each run. The reason is a counting argument. Reading the
coordinates in order, the only thing a threshold function must remember is how
many inputs have been one so far, so a node is determined by a position and a
running count, and there are $O(n^2)$ such pairs. This is the justification
offered in the main text for why the method climbs to 151 inputs, and it is a
statement about the $5{,}776$-node object, not about the $41{,}196{,}123$-node
allocation.

The two exponents answer different questions. The description exponent says
what it costs to \emph{hold} majority at a given width. The allocation exponent
says what it cost to \emph{recover} it from one particular eight-million-gate
circuit, and it would change if a different circuit computing the same function
were supplied. Only the description exponent is a property of the function.

\subsection{What the independence of the specification amounts to}

The check that the recovered object is majority compares the composed root
against the threshold builder called with every coordinate and a bound of
$\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1$. The canonical \textsc{majority} family in the gate
builder is defined by that same call under the default strict tie policy, so
naming the gate \textsc{majority} and matching the specification are one test,
not two. The measurement makes this visible:
\texttt{specification nodes} is zero at every size, because the specification
was already present in the shared store.

What remains, and is not circular, is the content that matters. The circuit is
produced by a recursive weight-merging construction that emits three-input
majority gates and shares no code with the threshold builder, which is a
dynamic program over coordinates. The identity therefore states that composing
that circuit yields the counting function, over all $2^n$ inputs at once. It
does not state that two independent specifications of majority were compared.

\subsection{The recovered name is a canonical choice, not a unique one}

At $n=31$ the recovery tests 162 candidates and two of them match the root
exactly: \textsc{majority} with default parameters, and \textsc{kofn} with
$k=16$. These are the same Boolean function, so both matches are correct and
neither can be eliminated by any amount of behavioural evidence. The reported
name is the earlier of the two in a fixed canonical priority order. The same
two-member match class occurs at every size in the ladder. What the method
identifies uniquely is the function; the name is a convention applied to it,
and the full match class is recorded rather than discarded.

\subsection{Consequence for the comparison in Section~S6}

The multiplier ladder used to bound Q8 counts allocation while composing a
circuit, with no recovery and no candidate gates. The like-for-like column for
majority is therefore its allocation, and that is the column the comparison
uses. Majority's allocation includes a recovery cost the multiplier's does not,
which inflates the majority side by about six per cent at $n=31$; this is far
below the gap being described, and the conclusion is unaffected. The
description sizes are reported beside the allocations so that the quadratic
growth of one function and the per-bit multiplicative growth of the other are
not read off columns that were produced by different procedures.
